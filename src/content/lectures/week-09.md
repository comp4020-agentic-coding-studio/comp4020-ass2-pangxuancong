This is the week most likely to be misread, so the boundary is drawn early:
nothing here is an argument for throwing. It is an argument that the cost of
dying is not fixed, and that a player who treats it as fixed will make a
predictable class of mistake.

The week is organised as a timeline, because the decision only exists inside
one.

## Before: the inherited assumption

Every player arrives with the same equation, learned on the Rift:

> death = lost time + lost pressure + gold handed to the enemy

On the Rift this is close enough to true to be worth treating as a rule,
because the Rift offers a cheaper way to reset. You walk home, you spend, you
come back. Recall converts gold into items at a cost measured in seconds of
lane presence.

This mode does not have that option. Spending is coupled to dying.

## The trigger: gold you cannot use

Some minutes into a game, a player is alive, healthy, and carrying enough gold
for a component that would meaningfully change what they can do in the next
fight. They cannot buy it. There is no route back to the shop that does not go
through the respawn timer.

Their inventory is now worth less than their bank. The team is fighting with a
member operating below the strength their gold has already paid for.

Call the difference between the two **stored purchasing power**: gold that has
been earned, cannot be spent, and is contributing nothing until it is.

## The decision window

The window is short and it is not always open. It opens when three things hold
at once:

1. stored purchasing power is high enough that spending it changes what the
   player can do, not merely their statistics
2. the death timer is short enough that the team can hold the position without
   them
3. the next fight is far enough away that they will be back for it, and near
   enough that arriving with the item matters

Miss any one and the calculation collapses. A death with an empty bank is a
death. A death thirty seconds before a fight the team then loses four-on-five
is a death. The window is real and it is narrow.

## The outcome: synchronised or staggered

Whether the window was used well is visible immediately in one property:
whether the team's deaths are **synchronised** or **staggered**.

Five players who die together return together, having all spent, and arrive as
a team. Five players who die one at a time return one at a time, each into a
fight already in progress against an enemy team at full strength. The same
five deaths, distributed differently, produce a coordinated reset in one case
and a sequence of donations in the other.

This is the actual skill in the week. Not deciding to die — deciding to die
*with the right people at the right time*, which is mostly a matter of not
being the one player who survived.

## The consequence: the window closes

Death timers lengthen as the game goes on. The same decision that is correct
early becomes indefensible late, and the transition is not signposted. A player
who learned that this play works and kept applying it will, at some point in
every game, apply it once too often.

The cost curve moves. The judgement has to move with it.

## The counterargument

Two, and both are serious.

**It is unfalsifiable in practice.** A player who dies badly can always narrate
it afterwards as a considered reset. The defence against this is the same as
everywhere else in the course: the reasoning has to be statable *before* the
outcome. If it could not have been written down beforehand, it was not a
decision.

**It licenses the wrong behaviour in the wrong hands.** True, and this is why
the boundary matters more than the principle. The difference between a
correct reset and inting is not intent, it is whether the three window
conditions held. A player who cannot name which of the three they checked was
not making this play.

## Takeaway

Death is not free and this week does not claim it is. It claims that the price
is variable, that the mode's purchasing constraint sometimes makes the price
worth paying, and that recognising the difference is a decision rather than an
excuse.
