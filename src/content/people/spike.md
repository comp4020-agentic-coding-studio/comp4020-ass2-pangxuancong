I teach decision-making in systems that constrain the decider. Most of that
work has been on games, because games are the only place where the constraints
are published, the outcomes are recorded, and nobody is harmed by studying
them carefully.

## Why this course exists

The literature on League of Legends is overwhelmingly about the part of the
game where players choose: draft theory, role identity, build paths, macro
rotation. ARAM is treated as the remainder — the mode you play while waiting
for friends, discussed in terms of which champions are strong and which
augments to take.

That framing is a category error, and it produces bad advice. A mode that
removes selection does not remove judgement; it moves judgement downstream,
from *what do I want* to *what is this for*. The second question is harder,
less documented, and almost never taught. Twelve weeks is about what it takes.

The course therefore spends very little time on rankings. A ranking is a
population-level summary, and the decision you are actually making is a
single-game one. The gap between those two things is where the semester lives.

## What I expect

An argument, not an answer. Every piece of assessment in this course is marked
on the reasoning that produced a decision, never on whether the decision
worked. A well-argued defence of a choice that lost the game receives full
marks; a correct choice defended by *it has the higher win rate* does not.

I expect claims to be traceable. If a number is patch-dependent, it carries a
patch. If a statement is about how the game is configured, it cites Riot. If it
is my reading rather than a measurement, I will say so, and you should too.
